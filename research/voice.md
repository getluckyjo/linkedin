# How Johannes actually writes

Built from his public journal, his site, his WhatsApp style, and a read of his own sent emails (phrasing from private threads lives in the Voice tab of the sheet, not here). The rule for every draft: use his sentences where he gave them, cut rather than add, and run the finished draft against the tells at the bottom. If a draft could have been written by anyone, it is not him.

## The shape of a Johannes message

- **One warm line, then straight in.** A greeting, one line of connection, then the point. He often labels it: "The short version:" or "Here's where it is."
- **Headline first, reasoning after.** The number or the outcome comes first, the why follows. Never a build-up to a reveal.
- **He diagnoses before he advises.** When he coaches, the first move is reframing the problem smaller: "your problem is narrower than it feels". Then one concrete mechanism, then a test the person can run themselves.
- **Asks plainly and invites pushback.** "Would you have 20 minutes next week?" "Shout while you're in it rather than after." "Tell me exactly where it's wrong and I'll fix it." No hedging.
- **Says the uncomfortable thing in print.** "We haven't yet solved our distribution strategy." "I'm not a lawyer or an accountant. I am an entrepreneur with a vision." Then moves on.
- **Corrects himself without fuss.** One line flagging the correction, the fix, done.
- **Numbered points when there are genuinely two or three things to check.** Not for lessons, not for rhythm. Each point is a paragraph with a mechanism in it, not a label.
- **Closes forward.** A next step, a time, "Speak soon", "Would love to hear your thoughts". Signs "Best," or "Groete," or just his name.

## Words and rhythm

- Short sentences with the odd fragment for weight. "That's the whole business." "Not a portfolio. Just what's actually there."
- Plain verbs: build, ship, scale, close, run, sort, fix, nudge, shout, wrap.
- South African English: keen, sort it, a quick nudge, the 19th, shout, flying ("hope the app is flying").
- Business terms used naturally: pitch, round, rev share, activation, ambassador, rate of sale, EBITDA. Never to impress.
- Em dashes appear in his emails, mid-sentence, as a breath. Allowed, sparingly. Commas and full stops do most of the work.
- Afrikaans with Afrikaans people, English business terms kept inside it: "reg vir sign-off voor ons live gaan", "nie 'n rand meer nie", "100% stem saam". Keep it that way.
- Numbers are specific and bare: "17 repositories", "32 storefronts", "R149 a month", "16 ads". Estimates get "about" or "sowat".
- Honest about failure and limits. Self-aware humour, lightly: "It is not a business. I mention it because..."
- He writes to one person, even in a group email. Posts should read the same: to one founder, not to "everyone".

## Tells that it is an AI version, not him (reject the draft)

- A tidy list of three "lessons" with parallel structure and punchy labels.
- "Here's what I learned", "game-changer", "thrilled", "humbled", "excited to announce", "watch this space", "let that sink in".
- A rhetorical question at the end to farm comments.
- Triplets for rhythm ("simple, honest, real").
- Explaining the lesson after the story. He tells the story, gives the mechanism, stops.
- Perfect polish throughout. His writing has the odd rough edge; leave one in.
- Adjectives doing the work. His writing works on nouns, verbs and numbers.
- Anything he would not say out loud at the 19th.

## How a post gets built from his words

1. Saturday he answers three questions in his own words (email reply, rough bullets, or a voice note transcript).
2. The draft keeps his sentences. Reorder, cut, fix spelling. Do not add a sentence he did not say unless it is a verified fact from `research/facts.md`.
3. The hook is his strongest sentence moved to the top, not a new one written for him.
4. End on his last real sentence or a plain next step. No manufactured question.
5. Read it aloud as a 40-something Cape Town founder talking to a mate who also runs a business. If any line would make that mate wince, cut it.
